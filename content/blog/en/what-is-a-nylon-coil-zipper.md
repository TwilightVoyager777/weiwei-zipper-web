A nylon zipper, also called a coil zipper, is a zipper whose teeth are formed by one continuous coil of plastic rather than by separate pieces. The coil is attached along the edge of each tape, and the two coils interlock when the slider passes. It is the lightest and most flexible of the three common zipper types.

That flexibility is why nylon zippers are everywhere in pockets, linings, bags, and light garments.

## How a coil zipper is built

The "teeth" of a coil zipper are the loops of a single plastic filament, called a monofilament, wound into a tight spiral. Each loop works as one tooth. The coil is then fixed to the tape, either sewn onto its edge or woven into it as the tape is made.

The name "nylon" comes from the material these zippers were first made from, and it has stuck as the name of the category. Today the coil may be made from nylon or from polyester; both work the same way and give the same flexible, low-profile chain.

Like other zippers, a coil zipper is finished with top and bottom stops and a slider, and an open-end version also has an insertion pin and box at the bottom.

## What nylon does well

**Flexibility.** Because the coil is one continuous piece, the chain bends easily in every direction. It follows curved seams and rounded corners, such as the corners of a bag or a pouch, which stiffer zippers struggle with.

**Light weight.** A coil chain is the lightest of the three types. It adds very little weight to light fabrics and does not pull them out of shape.

**Smooth running.** The slider glides along the coil with little resistance, which makes nylon zippers pleasant to use on items that are opened and closed often.

**Forgiving.** If part of a coil is pulled out of line, running the slider back over that section will often bring it back into place. A metal or resin tooth that bends or breaks cannot recover this way.

**Quiet look.** The coil sits low and is usually colored to match the tape, so the zipper blends into the product rather than standing out.

## Where it is less suited

Nylon gives up some strength and visual weight. On positions that take heavy load and abrasion, such as jean flies, workwear, and heavy-use bag openings, a [metal zipper](/products/metal-zippers) is usually the more durable choice. And if the zipper is meant to be a visible design feature with bold teeth, a [resin zipper](/blog/what-is-a-resin-zipper) says more.

The coil is also thermoplastic. Finished products should be pressed around the zipper or through a cloth, rather than with a hot iron directly on the coil.

## Common uses

* **Pockets and linings**, where the zipper should be light and discreet
* **Bags, pouches, and cases**, especially on curved seams and inner compartments
* **Sun-protective jackets and light outerwear**, where weight matters most
* **Dresses, skirts, and trousers**, where a flexible zipper follows the garment
* **Tents and outdoor gear**, where long, curved openings are common

## Conclusion

A nylon coil zipper uses one continuous plastic coil instead of separate teeth. It is light, flexible, smooth, and forgiving, which makes it the natural choice for pockets, linings, bags, and curved seams. Choose metal where the zipper takes heavy load, and resin where it should be a bold, visible detail.
